Who Regulates Space?

No single agency governs American activity in orbit. Jurisdiction is split across at least five, each holding one piece of a launch-to-reentry mission — and some novel activities fall through the cracks entirely.

FAA Office of Commercial Space Transportation (AST)

Licenses commercial launch, reentry, and spaceport operations to protect public safety, under 51 U.S.C. chapter 509. Safety requirements are consolidated in 14 CFR Part 450. The FAA's writ largely ends where orbit begins — it does not regulate what a spacecraft does once deployed.

Key authority: 51 U.S.C. ch. 509 · 14 CFR Part 450

FCC Space Bureau

Licenses the radio spectrum every satellite needs to communicate, which makes it a de facto regulator of satellite operations. FCC license conditions include orbital debris mitigation — notably the rule requiring low-Earth-orbit satellites to deorbit within five years of mission end.

Key authority: Communications Act · 47 CFR Part 25

NOAA Commercial Remote Sensing Regulatory Affairs

Licenses private Earth-imaging satellites under the Land Remote Sensing Policy Act. The 2020 reform sorted systems into tiers: the more widely available your imaging capability, the lighter the conditions on your license.

Key authority: 51 U.S.C. ch. 601 · 15 CFR Part 960

State & Commerce Export Controls (ITAR / EAR)

Space technology is treated as sensitive dual-use or defense technology. The State Department (ITAR) controls defense articles; the Commerce Department (EAR) controls most commercial satellite tech since the 2014 reform. Export compliance shapes who space companies can hire, partner with, and sell to.

Key authority: ITAR (22 CFR 120–130) · EAR (15 CFR 730–774)

DoD / U.S. Space Force + Commerce Office of Space Commerce

The military tracks objects in orbit and has long warned operators of potential collisions. That civil traffic-coordination role is transitioning to the Commerce Department's TraCSS system, leaving DoD focused on national security space.

Key authority: Space Policy Directive-3

The Mission Authorization Gap

Private space stations, satellite servicing, debris removal, lunar landers: novel activities with no clear licensing home. The Outer Space Treaty (Art. VI) obligates the U.S. to authorize and continuously supervise its nationals in space — but Congress hasn't assigned the job. Competing proposals would hand it to Commerce, the FAA, or split it. This is the live debate this site tracks.

Key authority: Outer Space Treaty, Art. VI — unimplemented for novel activities
